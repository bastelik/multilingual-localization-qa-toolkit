# Reviewer Calibration & Adjudication

Calibration helps reviewers apply the same quality criteria to the same evidence. The goal is not identical personal style; it is consistent identification and treatment of material defects.

## Before review begins

Provide every reviewer with the same:
- Project brief, target locale and audience definition
- Source files and reference materials
- Approved glossary and style guide
- Error taxonomy and severity definitions
- Instructions for queries, uncertainty and escalation
- Sample segments with agreed annotations

Clarify what reviewers are expected to assess and what is outside scope.

## Calibration exercise

1. Select a representative sample covering ordinary content and likely edge cases.
2. Ask reviewers to assess it independently before discussing it.
3. Compare issue identification, category, severity, evidence and proposed corrections.
4. Discuss disagreements by referring to the source, brief, glossary and applicable references.
5. Record the agreed decision and reasoning.
6. Add recurring or instructive cases to a shared precedent log.
7. Repeat with a fresh sample when guidance changes or inconsistency persists.

Include cases involving negation, modality, numbers, names, omissions, additions, terminology, register, locale conventions and ambiguous source text.

## A disciplined review method

For each suspected issue, ask:

- **Requirement:** What instruction, source meaning or quality criterion applies?
- **Evidence:** What exact text or reference supports the finding?
- **Comparison:** What differs between the expected result and the reviewed output?
- **Classification:** Which category best describes the defect?
- **Impact:** What is the likely consequence for the reader or task?
- **Action:** What correction, clarification or escalation is needed?

Use four evidence outcomes when helpful:
- **Supported:** The source or authoritative reference supports the target.
- **Contradicted:** The target conflicts with the source or applicable instruction.
- **Not stated:** The target introduces a claim not established by the available evidence.
- **Unclear:** The evidence is insufficient or ambiguous; further research or escalation is required.

“Not stated” is not automatically an error in every context. Determine whether the brief permits adaptation or explanatory additions.

## Adjudication

Escalate disagreements that affect meaning, safety, legal obligations, terminology policy or recurring team practice.

The adjudicator should:
1. Review the original source and full context, not only isolated comments.
2. Check the current brief, glossary, style guide and authoritative references.
3. Distinguish objective defects from acceptable alternatives.
4. Decide whether the issue is confirmed, rejected, reclassified or unresolved.
5. Record the rationale and any change to project guidance.
6. Share the precedent with the team when it is likely to recur.

Avoid resolving disputes by seniority or personal preference alone. Use the agreed evidence hierarchy.

## Monitoring consistency

Useful indicators include:
- Agreement on whether a defect exists
- Agreement on category and severity
- Rate of overturned findings after adjudication
- Recurring defect types by content or workflow
- Correction acceptance and rework patterns
- Calibration completion and time to resolve queries

Do not use a single metric as a complete measure of reviewer quality. Interpret results alongside task complexity, language pair, domain, sample size and reviewer experience.

## Feedback principles

Feedback should be specific, evidence-based and actionable. Identify the exact location, explain the applicable rule, show the impact and provide a correction or reference. Separate coaching from blame, and update shared guidance when a disagreement exposes a process gap.
